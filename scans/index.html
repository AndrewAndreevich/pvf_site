Introducing PVF Scan, a museum-grade record of art and the places it lives

PVF Scan turns the camera a gallery already owns into flat artworks in super resolution, sculpture in 3D, studios and exhibition halls you can walk, and the whole park as a 3D map, all from one pipeline

TBILISI, GEORGIA PVF today introduced PVF Scan, a scanning pipeline that gives an art institution a lasting record of its works and its spaces. With the camera the team already uses, PVF Scan makes flat, distortion-free images of paintings and graphics in super resolution, 3D scans of sculpture and objects, and walkable 3D scans of artist studios, exhibition halls and the open-air territory around them.

PVF Scan is designed first for LOCO Arts and LOCO Park in Tbilisi, where it becomes part of the new website: deep zoom on every artwork page, a 3D view for sculpture, a timeline for each studio and hall, and a 3D map of the park.

From a camera photo to a museum-grade scan

A catalogue photo is taken at an angle, in its frame, lit from the ceiling. One frame holds a few megapixels of the work. PVF Scan takes 10–20 close-up frames instead of one and stitches them into a single image that holds hundreds: the brushstroke, the palette-knife ridge and the weave of the canvas are all there.

The result is orthographic, with no perspective and no lens distortion, so the corners are square and the work looks exactly as it hangs on the wall. Because the scan knows the size of the work in centimetres, the artwork page shows a scale bar and a "view at real size" button.

Nobody crops or retouches by hand. Deep-zoom tiles, catalogue previews and card images are made automatically from every scan, and the work appears on the site on its own.

Sculpture and objects in 3D

Anything with volume is scanned all around: 60–100 frames in two or three rings at different heights, on a turntable or walking around the object. The photos become a 3D scene with Gaussian Splatting, which keeps soft materials, fabric and reflections looking real. Visitors turn the object on the artwork page, and a turntable and hero angles are rendered from the same scan for cards and the catalogue.

Progress, kept over time

A work can be scanned again at every stage. Each new scan is matched to the previous one automatically, so the work does not jump between stages, and a slider on the artwork page shows how it changed, with the date of every stage. It works the same for flat works and for objects.

Every studio, from artist to artist

Each studio is scanned empty, so an applicant can walk it in 3D before applying: size, light, walls and doors. It is scanned again when a resident moves in and when they leave, and the studio page gets a timeline of every residency. The trace each artist left in the room becomes part of the residency's history, linked to the artists' pages.

Every exhibition kept, and the park as a 3D map

The same scanning works at the scale of the place. Each hall is scanned with the show in it, 300–500 photos walking along the walls and through the middle, and visitors walk it from the exhibition page with the works in it as points. Scanned again before every show closes, a hall becomes a timeline of exhibitions, and the archive stops being a list of photos.

Outdoors, a drone flies the territory in parallel lines and ground photos fill in around buildings and outdoor works. The result is a 3D map of the whole park with navigation and walking routes, where every building and outdoor work opens its page. On slow devices a 2D map stays as a fallback.

How it works

Three kinds of scan, one way of working. The team learns it in one session on their own works.

    One pipeline, in the cloud or in the residence

    Who scans and where the scans are processed is set once for every kind of scan. One or two people from the team are trained, or the community joins in too: members scan their own works and upload them from their account, within a monthly quota or at a price per scan.

    Processing runs in the cloud, paid per scan, in a cheaper night queue from 3:00 to 9:00 or at once. Or it runs on a PC with a 16 TB NAS in the residence, where the sources stay and the computer works through the night for free; only the results go to the cloud. Visitors always load the scans from the cloud through a CDN, never from the residence.

    Pricing and availability

    • PVF Scan is available now as part of the LOCO Arts website, in modules that can be switched on one at a time. Work is billed at $40 an hour.
    • Processing in the cloud costs $3 a work in the night queue or $4 at once, and $7 or $10 for a studio or a hall. On a residence PC only the $1 handling per scan remains.
    • Storage of results and sources runs from about $7 to $35 a month, depending on volume.
    • The hybrid setup needs a PC (about $2,000) and a 16 TB NAS (about ₾3,500), bought once by the institution.
